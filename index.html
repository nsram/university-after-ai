A New Foundation for the University

Learning, judgment, and trust after AI

Sriram Natarajan

Drawing on Jason Potts, The University after AI.

Developed with assistance from ChatGPT and Grok.

Sources and speaker notes in companion file.

Who actually did this work?

2

A new gap

AI can be an extraordinarily patient tutor.

It can also produce a polished submission for a student who has learned very little.

Use AI to learn. Demand stronger evidence.

3

The thesis

As instruction and polished output become abundant, universities become more valuable where they can provide credible proof, valuable peers, real practice, and trusted judgment.

4

AI reaches farther

A university can tolerate cheap books and cheap lectures.

It faces a harder problem when essays, programs, analyses, and reports can be produced without clearly revealing what the student knows.

The evidence itself has changed.

5

A short history of abundance

Each technology wave changed the university differently.

→

6

Print

Printing made texts vastly easier to reproduce and circulate.

Universities responded with libraries, catalogs, curricula, and scholarly collections.

The degree still depended on selecting, teaching, examining, and certifying students.

7

Knight Library

A great library was once a serious academic technology.

The University of Oregon renamed its main library for the Knight family in 1988. Its expansion joined public funds with major private gifts.

Philanthropy followed scarcity.

8

Recorded teaching

Radio, television, film, VHS, and other media allowed lectures to travel beyond the room.

Before broadband video, a specialized recording could be genuinely hard to obtain.

Libraries became media collections too.

9

The university login

The web made search feel free. Serious academic literature often remained expensive.

Campus libraries bought access to JSTOR, ScienceDirect, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, PsycINFO, LexisNexis, Westlaw, and more.

10

Courses leave the campus

Around 2012, Coursera, edX, and Udacity made elite-branded courses available worldwide.

Some predicted that campuses would be displaced.

Instead, online courses became another layer of education. Distribution moved faster than credentialing.

11

Rock the JVM

The internet also created narrow specialists.

A learner can go to Rock the JVM for serious Scala and JVM craft, or to similar shops for one difficult skill.

That is valuable. It is still a smaller claim than a degree.

13

When the artifact stops being enough

The take-home artifact

A polished essay once bundled several clues together.

The student probably read, synthesized, wrote, revised, and spent time wrestling with the material.

Those inferences are becoming weaker.

14

One document, many histories

The same polished essay might now come from deep independent work, a long conversation with a model, heavy AI drafting, or minimal understanding plus good prompting.

The document alone cannot tell us enough.

15

Keep the essay

The essay may still teach.

Students can develop arguments, compare evidence, revise ideas, and receive feedback.

The narrower question is how much certification weight the final file should carry by itself.

16

Add attribution

After submission, ask:

Why this evidence?

What is the weakest part?

What would change your conclusion?

Apply the idea to a new case.

The conversation reveals what the PDF cannot.

17

Computer science

A repository can compile and pass every test without showing how much the student understands.

Open the code. Explain a function. Change one constraint. Predict what breaks. Repair it.

Now the code starts the assessment.

18

Laboratory science

A polished lab report is easy to improve after the experiment.

Capability is also visible in setup, instrument use, safety, anomaly detection, troubleshooting, and judgment about whether to repeat a result.

19

Medicine and nursing

Clinical competence is not just a written plan.

A patient changes. A medication has a risk. A family asks a hard question. A team must coordinate.

Simulation, supervised placement, and case defense show more than a report.

20

Art, design, performance

The final portfolio shows the result.

The process shows sketches, failed attempts, rehearsal, critique, material choices, and response to constraint.

Assessment can follow the work while it is still alive.

21

The work still matters. Its meaning grows when the student has to work with it again.

22

Closed-book exams help

A supervised exam can answer a useful question: can this person perform now, without outside assistance?

For some knowledge and problem-solving claims, that matters.

A degree usually claims more.

23

Match evidence to the claim

Knowledge recall may suit an exam.

Clinical judgment may require a simulation.

Programming may require live modification.

Design may require critique.

Research may require defense.

Teamwork may require observation over time.

24

Fairness matters more now

Observed assessment cannot become impressionistic assessment.

It needs clear criteria, calibrated examiners, attention to language and disability, protection against examiner variance, records of decisions, and appeals.

25

Bologna callback

Around 1088, Bologna grew from student initiative. Students gathered, set teaching goals, and paid teachers directly.

The early university already involved teaching, examination, and a trusted social arrangement.

AI makes that old core visible again.

27

The base

A strong course has a mind.

Someone decided what matters, what comes first, which examples illuminate the field, which misconceptions recur, and what students can ignore for now.

28

Buried intellectual capital

Universities already own lectures, demonstrations, problem sets, cases, notes, seminars, lab explanations, and student questions.

Much of it was built for a world where finding the right five minutes was difficult.

29

The content may still be good

A clear explanation of Fourier transforms may remain excellent years later.

A classic experiment may still be the right experiment.

A short segment inside an old lecture may be exactly what a student needs today.

30

The playlist is the problem

A forty-hour course no longer has to be consumed as forty consecutive hours.

Its concepts, examples, demonstrations, and explanations can become addressable pieces.

Students should meet the right part when they need it.

31

NPTEL

NPTEL is a vivid case.

The archive includes more than 70,000 hours of transcribed and subtitled video.

As an IIT Madras alumnus, I see both sides: the intellectual capital is enormous; the old playlist interface can hide it.

32

Curate before automating

Before a model remixes an archive, faculty can mark material as current, foundational, superseded, historically useful, optional, incorrect, or obsolete.

Curation protects the integrity of the base.

33

34

The next library may answer back

A library once helped a learner locate a source.

An AI-mediated academic archive can help the learner question it, connect it, practice with it, and return to the source when needed.

Institutional memory becomes interactive.

35

Remix

Once the base is trustworthy, personalization becomes much cheaper.

One student needs a simpler example. Another needs a harder problem. Another needs a visual explanation. Another needs a prerequisite.

The course bends without losing its spine.

36

Targeted renewal

Faculty do not need to rerecord forty hours every few years.

They can replace an obsolete section, add a better example, record a difficult explanation, flag a recurring misconception, or rethink the sequence.

37

Proof

At some point the institution makes a consequential claim:

This person can do the work.

AI can gather evidence and sharpen questions. Responsibility for the judgment remains identifiable and appealable.

38

Where faculty time matters most

AI can absorb much routine variation in explanation and practice.

Faculty attention becomes especially valuable in two places: shaping the course's intellectual architecture and making difficult judgments about capability.

39

What becomes more valuable

More than a course provider

If excellent explanation becomes widely available, students still need things information alone does not supply:

people worth learning beside

practice with consequences

mentors and examiners

access to institutions and professions

40

A cohort worth joining

An AI tutor can know the textbook.

It cannot become the classmate who challenges you, joins your research group, introduces you to an employer, or starts a company with you years later.

41

Cohorts need design

A cohort is not everyone admitted in the same year.

Its value comes from repeated interaction: shared work, complementary abilities, intellectual friction, rituals, projects, and continuity into alumni life.

42

Placement beside people

Universities put people beside others they could not easily find alone.

Peers, mentors, collaborators, employers, and funders become part of the learning environment.

When information is abundant, the quality of those connections matters more.

43

Practice under real constraints

Some learning becomes real only when the world pushes back.

A patient deteriorates. An instrument behaves strangely. A client changes the requirement. A team member disagrees. A performance goes wrong.

44

Where did the first rung go?

Many professions taught novices through useful but lower-risk work.

Junior programmers fixed small bugs. Young analysts built spreadsheets. New lawyers searched cases. Research assistants cleaned data.

Some of that work is becoming easier to automate.

45

The missing junior loop

If fewer beginners learn through routine work inside firms, someone still has to build the path from knowing to doing.

Universities could provide supervised residencies, simulation labs, apprenticeships, live projects, and professional studios.

46

A residency year

Imagine a final year organized around real constraints.

Students work on consequential projects under supervision. They meet in a cohort. They explain decisions as they make them. Their final work is assessed through performance and defense.

47

New fields move faster than committees

AI agents. Robotics. Synthetic biology. Data governance. Cybersecurity. Drones.

By the time a slow curriculum process approves a program, the frontier may have moved.

48

Fast credentials

A university already has two things many new providers lack: recognized standing and disciplinary experts who can judge quality.

A standing unit could create, examine, review, and retire short credentials quickly.

49

Research changes too

AI lowers the cost of search, coding, analysis, drafting, and routine synthesis.

That can accelerate science. It may also pull attention toward questions that are easier to formulate, measure, and optimize.

50

Go where the data are thin

Universities have a reason to pursue work with uncertain payoff, little training data, long horizons, unfashionable questions, negative results, new instruments, and poorly framed problems.

The frontier remains valuable because it resists prediction.

51

Not the same storm

Elite institutions have brand and cohort protection.

Specialist institutions are stronger where labs, clinics, licensure, or deep physical expertise matter.

Broad teaching universities may face the hardest choices when instruction and coordination become easier to buy elsewhere.

52

Institutional design

How independent should certification become?

Instructor teaches and grades
Second examiner moderates
Assessment office examines
Separate body certifies

Different claims may need different levels of distance.

53

Who is accountable for the pass?

AI may perform much of the evidence gathering.

For consequential certification, a named person or accountable academic body should be able to explain the decision, defend the standard, and hear an appeal.

54

Administration after AI

Some administrative work protects the institution's core claims: records, appeals, assessment integrity, research ethics, safety, licensure, and legal standing.

Other work can be automated, shared, simplified, or purchased.

55

A running university cannot simply stop

Current students still need to graduate. Existing programs remain accredited. Research continues. Faculty commitments persist. Donor promises remain. Employers expect continuity.

That makes wholesale redesign difficult even when the case is strong.

56

Build the next model at the edge

A new school, college, credentialing arm, residency program, or assessment unit can make a smaller set of new promises.

Give it a budget, a timetable, academic standards, and room to fail safely.

Core universitykeeps faith
Edge unittests the next model

60

Measure what outsiders care about

Can graduates do what the credential says?

Do employers trust the assessment?

Can students demonstrate capability beyond a polished artifact?

Do strong students want the cohort?

What does serious verification cost?

61

The claim

At graduation, the university should know more than whether a student completed the required courses.

It should be able to say: we saw this person reason, make, recover from mistakes, and work under constraints.

We know what this graduate can do.

Appendix A1: Glossary

Trusted claim / warrant: the public claim that a graduate has the capability represented by the credential.

Signal erosion: familiar evidence separates people less clearly.

Base: expert-curated intellectual architecture.

Remix: AI-supported adaptation.

Observed proof: evidence attributable to the learner.

65

Appendix A2: Protected judgment

People making consequential academic judgments must be able to say no.

An examiner should be able to fail a candidate or reject inadequate work despite pressure from enrollment, rankings, revenue, employers, or families.

66

Appendix A3: Hart asset

A Hart asset is an asset the organization cannot safely let another party control.

Main deck translation: what must the university continue to control if everything else becomes easier to buy?

67

Appendix A4: Sharper certification model

A more radical model separates instruction and certification more strongly.

Students could learn through university teaching, AI systems, external courses, work experience, or independent study. The university would concentrate on examination and certification.

68

Appendix A5: Affiliation without traditional employment

AI may reduce the infrastructure needed for some independent scholarship.

Universities may still provide grant holding, ethics review, laboratories, legal standing, colleagues, students, and reputation.

69

Appendix A6: Strategic contraction

Which relationships genuinely require a university?

Activities based largely on information transfer may have strong external substitutes. Activities requiring legal standing, physical presence, durable trust, professional accountability, or certification may justify internal capacity.

70

Appendix A7: Outcome financing

Outcome-linked financing raises practical, ethical, political, and regulatory questions.

Any entity responsible for independent certification should be cautious about holding a financial interest in the outcomes it certifies.

71
